Periodical and Journal copyright rules by country

| Country       | Initial Ownership                                                                 | Common Practice in Periodicals and Journals                                      | Notes                                                                 |
|---------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------|
| United States | The copyright initially vests in the author, who is the creator of the original work. For collective works such as periodicals, the publisher owns the copyright in the collective work (selection and arrangement), while the author retains copyright in their individual contribution unless transferred. | Authors typically sign a copyright transfer agreement, assigning their rights to the publisher as a condition of publication. This allows the publisher to control reproduction, distribution, and other uses. | In cases of work made for hire, such as employed journalists, the employer (publisher) owns the copyright. Freelance authors retain ownership unless they agree to transfer it. |
| Canada        | The author is the initial owner of the copyright, unless the work is created in the course of employment, in which case the employer owns it. | For contributions to newspapers, magazines, or periodicals, freelancers retain ownership unless agreed otherwise, but publishers own the compilation. Authors often transfer rights to publishers via agreements, though retention of rights is encouraged. | Moral rights remain with the author even after assignment of economic rights. Journalists retain the right to restrain publication outside the periodical context. |
| United Kingdom| The first owner of copyright is usually the author or, if created in the course of employment, the employer. | Authors commonly assign copyright to the publisher through a publishing agreement, granting the publisher exclusive rights. In some cases, authors may retain copyright and grant licenses. | For commissioned works, ownership is determined by the contract. Joint authorship results in shared ownership. |
| France        | The author, as the natural person who created the work, is the initial owner. | Economic rights may be transferred to the publisher, while moral rights remain perpetual and inalienable with the author. Press publishers have related rights for online use of publications. | Only natural persons can be authors; legal entities exercise rights for anonymous or collective works. |
| Germany       | Copyright vests in the creator, who must be a natural person; companies cannot own copyright. | Copyright itself is inalienable, but authors grant exclusive exploitation rights or licenses to publishers. Publishers may share in remuneration claims if rights are granted to them. | Independent contractors retain ownership; joint works are co-owned. Exclusive licenses may revert to non-exclusive after 10 years in certain cases. |
