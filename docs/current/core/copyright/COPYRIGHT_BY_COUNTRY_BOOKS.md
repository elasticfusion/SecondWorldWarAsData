Copyright laws by country

| United States | Copyright Act of 1976, codified in Title 17 of the United States Code.                | Original works of authorship fixed in a tangible medium, including literary, dramatic, musical, artistic, pictorial, graphic, sculptural, motion pictures, sound recordings, and architectural works. | For works created by individuals, the author's life plus 70 years; for works made for hire, 95 years from publication or 120 years from creation, whichever is shorter. | Protection is automatic upon fixation, but registration with the U.S. Copyright Office is required to file infringement lawsuits and to seek statutory damages or attorney's fees. | Limited moral rights, primarily under the Visual Artists Rights Act (VARA) for works of visual art, including rights of attribution and integrity. | Fair use doctrine under Section 107, evaluated based on four factors: purpose and character of use, nature of the copyrighted work, amount used, and effect on the market. Other exceptions include educational uses and library archiving. |
| Canada        | Copyright Act (R.S.C., 1985, c. C-42).                                                | Original literary, dramatic, musical, and artistic works, including books, music, paintings, sculptures, computer programs, and films. | Generally, the author's life plus 70 years from the end of the calendar year of death.          | Protection is automatic upon creation and fixation; registration with the Canadian Intellectual Property Office is voluntary but provides evidentiary benefits in disputes. | Moral rights include the right to integrity of the work and attribution, which are inalienable but can be waived; they endure for the same term as economic rights. | Fair dealing for purposes such as research, private study, education, parody, satire, criticism, review, and news reporting; additional exceptions for non-commercial user-generated content and educational institutions. |
| United Kingdom| Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988 (CDPA).                                       | Original literary, dramatic, musical, and artistic works; sound recordings, films, broadcasts, and typographical arrangements. | For literary, dramatic, musical, and artistic works, the author's life plus 70 years; shorter terms for sound recordings (70 years from publication) and broadcasts (50 years). | Protection is automatic upon creation; no formal registration system exists.               | Moral rights include paternity (attribution), integrity (protection against derogatory treatment), false attribution, and privacy in certain photographs and films; these can be waived but not assigned. | Fair dealing for criticism, review, quotation, news reporting, research, private study, and parody; additional exceptions for education, libraries, and archives. |
| France        | Intellectual Property Code (Code de la propriété intellectuelle).                     | Original works of the mind, including literary, artistic, musical, graphic, plastic, software, and applied arts, provided they exhibit originality. | Economic rights last for the author's life plus 70 years; moral rights are perpetual.           | Protection is automatic upon creation; no registration is required, though deposit options exist for evidentiary purposes. | Strong moral rights, which are perpetual, inalienable, and imprescriptible, including rights of paternity, integrity, disclosure, and withdrawal. | Exceptions include private and family use, parody, pastiche, caricature, analyses and short quotations for criticism or review, and educational uses; influenced by EU directives. |
| Germany       | Act on Copyright and Related Rights (Urheberrechtsgesetz – UrhG).                     | Literary, scientific, and artistic works, including writings, speeches, computer programs, musical works, pantomimes, choreographic works, photographic works, and cinematographic works. | Generally, the author's life plus 70 years.                                                    | Protection is automatic upon creation; no formal registration is necessary.                | Moral rights include recognition of authorship and protection against distortion or mutilation of the work; these are inalienable. | Limitations for teaching (up to 15% of a work), scientific research (up to 15% for non-commercial purposes), quotations, incidental inclusions, and public domain uses; recent amendments enhance exceptions for education and research. |
